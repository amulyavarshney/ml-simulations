Session 1

PCA in practice

Find the direction of maximum variance

PC1 is the unit direction u that maximises the variance of the scores u·x. Equivalently it minimises the squared distance left over, because kept + lost = total. It is the eigenvector of the covariance matrix with the largest eigenvalue λ₁, which equals the variance along it. The SVD of centred data gives the same answer: λ = s²/(n−1). Share = λ / Σλ.

Picture

Shadows on a line. PC1 is the line where the points' shadows spread out the most. What the shadow hides is the distance from each point to the line.

Formula in words

kept + lost = total, at every angle (Pythagoras). So "maximise the spread" and "minimise the leftover distance" are one instruction. In the widget, "Kept + lost" never moves.

Rule of thumb

An eigenvalue is the variance along its direction, and the eigenvalues add up to the total variance (a + c in 2-D). The share of PC j is λⱼ / Σλ.

Sanity check

PC1 and PC2 are exactly 90° apart: on the right plot, the trough is 90° after the peak. If λ₁ and λ₂ are close, do not read meaning into PC1.

Trick

The sign of a PC is arbitrary: PC1 and −PC1 are the same line. When two programs disagree on signs, flip one. Compare angles modulo 180° or absolute values.

Link to code

np.linalg.eigh(C): eigenvectors are columns, smallest eigenvalue first. np.linalg.svd(Xc): PCs are rows of Vt, and s**2/(n-1) are the eigenvalues. PCA().fit(X): components_ (rows) and explained_variance_.

Code to remember: Covariance matrix and eigenvectors by hand

import numpy as np

X = np.array([[18, 9], [19, 9], [20, 11], [21, 11], [22, 10]])  # income, spend (Rs thousand)
Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)               # centre first: PCA looks at spread around the mean
C = Xc.T @ Xc / (len(X) - 1)          # 2x2 covariance matrix
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(C)        # eigh = eigen for symmetric matrices, smallest first

print("covariance:", C.tolist())
print("eigenvalues:", vals[::-1].round(3))
print("PC1:", np.abs(vecs[:, -1]).round(3), "share:", round(vals[-1] / vals.sum(), 3))

Scaling decides what PCA sees (wine)

PCA maximises variance, and variance carries the unit squared (a column times m has variance times m²). Raw wine: proline (sd 314) takes 99.8% of PC1, purity 67%. Standardise with z = (x − mean)/sd, so every column has variance 1 and PCA uses the correlation matrix: PC1 36.2%, purity 93%. Skip it only for columns sharing a meaningful unit.

Rule of thumb

Variance has the unit squared. Multiply a column by m and its variance is multiplied by m². Milligrams to grams (m = 0.001) divides the variance by a million.

Sanity check

Before trusting a PCA, look at the sd of each column and the largest loading of PC1. One sd 10 times bigger than the rest, or one loading near ±1, means a unit is speaking, not the data. The page turns that readout amber for you.

Rule of thumb

Different units (rupees, years, mg per litre): standardise. One shared unit whose size matters (pixels, same sensor): think first, often leave it raw.

Formula in words

z = (x − mean) / sd. After it every column has mean 0 and variance 1, so the total variance is the number of columns (13 for wine) and each PC's share is its eigenvalue divided by 13.

Picture

The log-scale bar chart of sd is the quickest diagnostic. Bars about equal: scaling will change little. One bar sticking out: that column will take PC1.

Link to code

make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA(n_components=2)). The scaler learns its means and sds from the rows you fit on, which in cross-validation are the training rows only.

Code to remember: Raw versus standardised PCA on the wine data

from sklearn.datasets import load_wine
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

data = load_wine()
X = data.data
Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)        # every column: mean 0, sd 1

    pca = PCA().fit(M)
    top = abs(pca.components_[0]).argmax()    # feature with the largest |loading| on PC1
    print(name, "PC1 share:", round(pca.explained_variance_ratio_[0], 3), "| top feature:", data.feature_names[top])

Scree plot, cumulative variance and choosing k (digits)

The share of component j is λⱼ/Σλ, and cum(k) is the variance that k components keep. Choose the smallest k with cum(k) ≥ target (digits: 21, 29, 41 for 90, 95, 99%) or cross-validate the real task. Rebuild with x̂ = mean + Σⱼ≤k sⱼvⱼ. Squared error left ÷ total = 1 − cum(k), exactly.

Picture

The cumulative curve is a price list. Read it left to right ("k numbers keep this much") or right to left ("this much needs k numbers"). It climbs steeply at first and flattens: early components are cheap, late ones are expensive.

Formula in words

Error left = 1 − cumulative variance. A 95% target means you accept 5% of the squared error. The page's two readouts prove it.

Trick

In code: np.argmax(cum >= target) + 1. argmax finds the first True, and the +1 is because Python counts from 0 while k counts from 1. Forgetting it gives 28 instead of 29, and 94.99% instead of 95.48%.

Sanity check

The shares must add to 100%, and cum(64) = 100%. Here three pixels are blank in every image, so the last three eigenvalues are 0. Only 61 directions carry any variance, and the curve is exactly 100% from k = 61 on.

Rule of thumb

Check the task, not only the variance. A logistic regression on the first k components, scored by 5-fold cross-validation, gets 57% at k = 2, 89% at k = 10, 91.0% at k = 29 and 91.3% with all 64 pixels. A 95% target keeps nearly all the accuracy, and 74% (k = 10) loses about 2.5 points.

Link to code

PCA(n_components=0.95): a float below 1 is a variance target, an integer is k itself. pca.n_components_ tells you what it chose.

Code to remember: Variance per component and the k for each target

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

X = load_digits().data                    # 1797 images, 64 pixels each
pca = PCA().fit(X)                        # keep all 64 components, ranked by variance
cum = np.cumsum(pca.explained_variance_ratio_)   # running total of the shares
print("first 5 shares (%):", (100 * pca.explained_variance_ratio_[:5]).round(2))
for target in (0.90, 0.95, 0.99):
    k = int(np.argmax(cum >= target)) + 1  # first position where the target is reached
    print(f"target {target}: k = {k}, kept {cum[k - 1]:.4f}")

Reconstruct and denoise digits

Noise adds σ² to every direction, while digits keep their variance λⱼ in a few. Keeping k components leaves an error per pixel of about (Σⱼ>k λⱼ + k·σ²)/64: signal thrown away plus noise kept, a U-shape. The minimum is near λⱼ ≈ σ², so keep components with λⱼ > σ². More noise, smaller best k. A fixed variance target ignores σ.

Formula in words

error ≈ (signal thrown away + noise kept) ÷ 64, that is (Σⱼ>k λⱼ + k·σ²) / 64. The first part falls with k, the second rises, and the U-shape is their sum.

Rule of thumb

Keep component j only while λⱼ > σ². Counting clean eigenvalues above σ², for σ = 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 you get 47, 33, 16, 10, 5. The page's best k for its first noise sample is 48, 33, 15, 10, 4.

Picture

Left arm of the U: blur, from too few components. Right arm: speckle, from too many. The bottom is flat. At σ = 4, k = 12 (error 7.27) is within 2% of k = 15 (7.15).

Sanity check

At k = 64 the error must equal the noisy input's, because nothing is removed. If your own denoising does not satisfy this, something else was changed (for example clipping the pixels).

Trick

You can estimate σ² from the tail of the noisy scree plot. At σ = 4, the last 20 eigenvalues of the noisy data average 14.8 against σ² = 16. It is a little low because the smallest eigenvalues of a sample are always pulled down.

Link to code

pca.inverse_transform(pca.transform(noisy)) compresses then expands: that is denoising. For a whole curve, fit once with all 64 components and slice: Z[:, :k] @ pca.components_[:k] + pca.mean_.

Code to remember: Add noise, denoise with k components, measure the error

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

X = load_digits().data                              # clean images, grey levels 0 to 16
noisy = X + 4 * np.random.RandomState(0).normal(size=X.shape)   # noise with sigma = 4 on every pixel
print("noisy input:", round(float(((noisy - X) ** 2).mean()), 1))
for k in (1, 5, 15, 30, 64):
    pca = PCA(n_components=k).fit(noisy)            # learn directions from the NOISY images
    den = pca.inverse_transform(pca.transform(noisy))   # keep k directions, rebuild 64 pixels
    print("k =", k, "error vs clean:", round(float(((den - X) ** 2).mean()), 1))

Tune k by cross-validation, inside the pipeline (wine)

Pipeline = scale → PCA(k) → classifier, every step fitted on the training folds only; held-out folds are only transformed. 5-fold CV gives a mean and a spread per k. Wine: k = 1 gives 84.9%, best k = 11 (98.9 ± 1.4), simplest within one spread k = 6 (97.8). Scaling or PCA on all rows first is a leak, even when harmless, as here.

Picture

Five exams. Each fold is an exam paper locked away while the pipeline (scaler, PCA, model) studies from the other four. The exam papers never touch the studying.

Rule of thumb

Fit on train, transform on test. Anything with a fit belongs inside the pipeline. The held-out fold only ever goes through transform and predict.

Sanity check

To audit for leakage, ask of every step: "Was its fit ever called on rows that include the held-out fold?" One yes is a leak, even for steps that never look at labels.

Rule of thumb

Differences smaller than the spread are noise. One wine is 0.56 points of 178, so a spread of ±1.4 is about two and a half wines.

Formula in words

Mean ± spread over the folds for each k. Take the smallest k with mean ≥ (best mean − best spread).

Sanity check

Always compare with the dumbest baseline. Always answering the biggest cultivar gets 39.9% here. A score near that means the model learned nothing.

Link to code

GridSearchCV(pipe, {"pca__n_components": range(1, 14)}, cv=5). The double underscore means "the step named pca, its parameter n_components".

Code to remember: Cross-validate the whole pipeline for a few values of k

from sklearn.datasets import load_wine
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_wine(return_X_y=True)
for k in (1, 2, 6, 11, 13):
    pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA(n_components=k), LogisticRegression(max_iter=10000, tol=1e-8))
    s = cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5)       # five times: fit on 4 folds, score on the 5th
    print(k, round(s.mean(), 4), round(s.std(), 4))
Session 2

Nonlinear embeddings: t-SNE and UMAP

A linear map cannot unroll a curved sheet

Data on a curved sheet cannot be flattened by a straight projection: PCA drops a direction and piles layers up. t-SNE and UMAP keep only local neighbour relations, which are safe while a neighbourhood is smaller than the gap between layers (6.3 here), so they unroll the sheet but may cut it and place pieces anywhere. Trustworthiness (10 neighbours) catches false neighbours, not cuts.

Picture

An ant on the carpet. Neighbours should be the places the ant reaches in a few steps, not what a bird sees across the layers.

Rule of thumb

Straight-line distance is trustworthy only inside a small patch. Keep the neighbourhood smaller than the gap between layers (6.3 here: the 10th neighbour was 4.2 away at 350 points).

Sanity check

Colour the map by something you know (here the position t). Smooth colour change along a strand means it is unrolled. Crossing bands mean it is flattened or glued.

Formula in words

Trustworthiness = 1 minus (a penalty for every false neighbour, bigger the further down the 3-D list it was). It counts intruders, not cuts.

Rule of thumb

In an unrolled map only who is next to whom is real. Flips, turns, gaps and the order of the strands are free choices of the method.

Code to remember: The straight line against the walk along the sheet

import numpy as np

t = np.array([6.0, 6.0 + 2 * np.pi])        # sheet positions: a point, and the same spot one turn further out
xz = np.c_[t * np.cos(t), t * np.sin(t)]    # the roll's x and z coordinates (same height y, so y cancels)

straight = np.linalg.norm(xz[1] - xz[0])    # straight-line distance in 3-D
along = (t[1] ** 2 - t[0] ** 2) / 2         # length of sheet between them: the spiral r = t
print("straight line:", round(straight, 2))
print("along the sheet:", round(along, 1))
print("ratio:", round(along / straight, 1))

t-SNE: perplexity sets each point's neighbourhood

t-SNE gives each point a Gaussian over its neighbours and tunes the width σ per point until the perplexity eH equals the setting, i.e. about that many neighbours count. The map copies these weights (symmetrised, with a Student-t in 2-D) by minimising KL. KL depends on the perplexity, so compare settings with trustworthiness and purity. Too low fragments clusters, too high merges them.

Picture

Perplexity is the number of friends who count. σ is how far you must look to find them: short in a hostel, long in a village.

Formula in words

Perplexity = eentropy of the weights. If the weight is shared equally among k points, it is exactly k. That is why it reads as a neighbour count.

Rule of thumb

Pick a perplexity below the size of the smallest group you care about and well below n. Typical range 5 to 50. Run three values and trust only what survives all three.

Sanity check

After the search, "Perplexity achieved" must equal the target. If it does not, the value was capped or the search failed, and the map answers a different question than you asked.

Rule of thumb

Never rank settings by KL. Compare trustworthiness and purity, and look at the maps.

Link to code

TSNE(perplexity=30) sets the target. After fit_transform, tsne.kl_divergence_ holds the final KL.

Code to remember: Perplexity of one point, from its distances

import numpy as np

d = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0])            # distances from point i to its four neighbours

def perplexity(sigma):
    w = np.exp(-d**2 / (2 * sigma**2))         # Gaussian weight of each neighbour
    p = w / w.sum()                            # conditional probabilities p(j|i): they add to 1
    H = -(p * np.log(p)).sum()                 # entropy of that distribution, in nats
    return np.exp(H), p                        # perplexity = e^H = "effective number of neighbours"

for s in (1, 2, 3, 4):
    print("sigma", s, "-> perplexity", round(perplexity(s)[0], 2))
print("p at sigma 3:", np.round(perplexity(3)[1], 3))

t-SNE: same data, different seeds

Different seeds give different random starts and so different t-SNE layouts of equal quality. Neighbourhoods repeat (trustworthiness within about 0.01), but the spacing between clusters does not (mean agreement r about 0.4 to 0.75). Rotation or flip alignment cannot fix that. PCA start makes runs identical without making the layout meaningful. Report only what survives three seeds.

Picture

Wedding seating: families together (reliable), tables in a different order each time (arbitrary).

Rule of thumb

Trust who is next to whom inside and between near clumps. Do not trust the distance between far clusters, their order or the orientation.

Trick

Rerun with 3 seeds. A statement that appears in all three is a finding. A statement that appears in one is decoration.

Sanity check

Before writing "A is closer to B than to C", change the seed. If the claim flips, it came from the seed, not from the data.

Formula in words

Agreement r = correlation of the 45 centre-to-centre distances of two maps. It ignores scale and is unchanged by rotation, flips and shifts.

Link to code

TSNE(random_state=s) sets the seed. init="pca" makes the start (and so the result) the same for every seed.

Code to remember: Same neighbours, different spacing

import numpy as np
from itertools import combinations
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.manifold import TSNE, trustworthiness

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)
pick = np.random.RandomState(0).choice(len(y), 300, replace=False)   # 300 random images
X, y = X[pick], y[pick]
centres = lambda E: np.array([E[y == k].mean(axis=0) for k in range(10)])        # one centre per digit
gaps = lambda E: [np.linalg.norm(a - b) for a, b in combinations(centres(E), 2)]  # the 45 centre gaps
runs = [TSNE(init="random", method="exact", random_state=s).fit_transform(X) for s in range(3)]
print("every run trusted (> 0.95):", all(trustworthiness(X, E, n_neighbors=10) > 0.95 for E in runs))
r = [np.corrcoef(gaps(runs[i]), gaps(runs[j]))[0, 1] for i, j in combinations(range(3), 2)]
print("mean agreement of centre gaps below 0.8:", np.mean(r) < 0.8)

Reading plots honestly: sizes, gaps and structure from nothing

Maps are built from neighbour weights that are divided by each point's local scale, so loose and tight clusters look alike and apparent size follows head-count (240 against 40 customers looked 4 times wider). Gaps between far clusters are redrawn too. Small perplexity makes clumps in pure noise. Measure size and distance in the features; run a null test and several seeds.

Rule of thumb

The map communicates; the original space measures. Compute spreads and distances from the features, then draw the map to show them.

Picture

A metro map: right about order and connections, wrong about kilometres.

Trick

Null test. Shuffle each column independently (this keeps every feature's distribution but kills the structure), rerun with the same settings and compare. Clumps that appear in both are artefacts.

Sanity check

Divide the typical nearest-neighbour distance by the typical distance. With many features, pure noise gives about 0.76 (50 features), the digits about 0.37 and well-separated tight clusters 0.1 or less. A value near 0.76 says "no clusters to find". It is meaningful only with many features.

Rule of thumb

Equal-looking clusters in a map do not mean equal clusters in the data. Head-count shows up as size, loose and tight look the same.

Link to code

True spread of cluster k: np.linalg.norm(X[y==k] - X[y==k].mean(0), axis=1).mean(), computed on X, never on the map.

Code to remember: Stretching a cluster does not change the weights

import numpy as np

def p_row(d, sigma):                           # conditional probabilities of one point, as in t-SNE
    w = np.exp(-d**2 / (2 * sigma**2))
    return w / w.sum()

tight = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0])         # distances to four neighbours in a tight cluster
loose = 4 * tight                              # the same cluster stretched 4 times: a loose cluster
print(np.round(p_row(tight, 3), 3))
print(np.round(p_row(loose, 12), 3))           # the perplexity search finds a sigma 4 times larger
print("identical:", np.allclose(p_row(tight, 3), p_row(loose, 12)))

UMAP: n_neighbors and min_dist

UMAP builds a graph: each point links to its n_neighbors nearest, nearest = strength 1, others exp(−(d − ρ)/σ) with σ chosen so strengths add to log₂(n_neighbors), two directions merged as a + b − ab. n_neighbors alone sets this graph. min_dist only sets how tightly the layout packs points (curve parameters a and b). Small n_neighbors: detail, fragments. Large: broad, blurred.

Picture

Friends list and poster. n_neighbors = how many friends each student lists. min_dist = how close the dots may be drawn on the poster.

Rule of thumb

n_neighbors is what you believe about the data. min_dist is how you draw it. If the graph readouts (edges, cross-class share) did not move, you changed only the drawing.

Rule of thumb

Start at n_neighbors = 15 and min_dist = 0.1. Try 5 and 50 and keep what both show. For a clustering step use min_dist 0, for a picture 0.5 or more.

Formula in words

Nearest neighbour = strength 1. Others = exp(−(extra distance)/σ). Two directions merge as a + b − ab.

Sanity check

Links per point should be about n_neighbors to 1.2 times n_neighbors. If it is far below, or trustworthiness collapses (about 0.7 at n_neighbors = 2), the graph has shattered. Raise n_neighbors.

Link to code

UMAP(n_neighbors=15, min_dist=0.1, random_state=0). After fit, .graph_ holds the links and .embedding_ the map.

Code to remember: Strengths of one point's links

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

d = np.array([1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 2.5])            # distances to the 4 neighbours (n_neighbors = 5 counts the point itself)
rho = d.min()                                  # nearest neighbour is always fully connected: strength 1
target = np.log2(5)                            # strengths must add up to log2(n_neighbors) = 2.32
member = lambda s: np.exp(-(d - rho) / s)      # strength of each neighbour for a given sigma
sigma = brentq(lambda s: member(s).sum() - target, 0.01, 100)    # find the sigma that hits the target
print("rho", rho, " sigma", round(sigma, 2))
print("memberships", np.round(member(sigma), 2), " sum", round(member(sigma).sum(), 2))
a, b = member(sigma)[2], 0.30                  # P's view of Q is 0.47; Q's view of P is 0.30
print("edge weight a + b - ab =", round(a + b - a * b, 2))

UMAP places new points; t-SNE cannot

UMAP.transform places a new point by finding its nearest training points in the original space, giving them fuzzy strengths, starting at their weighted average map position and polishing for 100 rounds with the training points frozen. t-SNE has no transform: a refit moves old points (about a quarter of the map width here). Fit the map on training rows only, then transform the test rows.

Picture

Street index against redrawing the whole map. UMAP looks the new house up. t-SNE draws a new map.

Rule of thumb

Anything whose output feeds a model must be a function you can apply to new rows: fit once, transform later. PCA and UMAP are. t-SNE is a picture, not a feature maker.

Sanity check

After transform, the training embedding must be unchanged: np.allclose(before, after) is True. If it is not, you refitted by accident.

Trick

Always score the same classifier on the original features as a baseline. Here 95% on pixels against about 94% on 2 coordinates: the map lost almost nothing.

Rule of thumb

Fit the map on the training rows only, or put it in a Pipeline so that cross-validation refits it inside every fold.

Formula in words

New position = strength-weighted average of the neighbours' map positions, then 100 small corrections.

Code to remember: Fit UMAP on training digits, place unseen digits, classify

import numpy as np, umap
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X[:1000], y[:1000], test_size=0.3, random_state=0)

um = umap.UMAP(n_neighbors=15, random_state=0).fit(X_tr)       # learn the map from training digits only
E_tr = um.embedding_.copy()
E_te = um.transform(X_te)                                       # place unseen digits on that map
knn = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=10).fit(E_tr, y_tr)
print("test shape:", E_te.shape, " accuracy above 0.9:", knn.score(E_te, y_te) > 0.9)
print("training points moved:", not np.allclose(E_tr, um.embedding_))

SVM I: maximum margin

The widest street on a number line

Margin of a threshold = distance to the nearest reading. Best threshold = midpoint of the two closest opposite readings: t = (a + b)/2, margin (b − a)/2. The SVM writes the same thing as f(x) = w·x + b with threshold −b/w, margin 1/|w|, street 2/|w|. One odd reading removes the hard margin; C prices the broken rule.

Picture

Find the gap, stand in the middle. The two readings touching the gap are the only ones that matter: the rings.

Formula in words

Best threshold = average of the two closest opposite readings, (a + b)/2. Margin = half the gap between them, (b − a)/2.

Sanity check

At the best threshold the distances to the nearest reading on the left and on the right are equal. If they differ (0.2 and 0.7, say), slide the threshold towards the farther side until they match.

Rule of thumb

Margin = how much a reading can wiggle before its label flips. Margin 0.45 means measurement noise up to 0.45 on the nearest readings is harmless.

Trick

Margin is the half, street is the whole. Street = 2 × margin = 2/|w|. On one feature with a hard margin, the street fills the gap exactly (0.9 here).

Link to code

-svm.intercept_[0] / svm.coef_[0, 0] is the threshold and 1 / abs(svm.coef_[0, 0]) is the margin. Both come from the same two numbers w and b.

Code to remember: Find the best threshold by hand: the midpoint of the closest pair

import numpy as np

x = np.array([-2.1, -1.7, -1.4, -1.0, -0.7, -0.4, 0.5, 0.9, 1.2, 1.6, 2.0, 2.3])
y = np.array([-1] * 6 + [1] * 6)              # -1 healthy (left), +1 failing (right)

a, b = x[y == -1].max(), x[y == 1].min()      # the closest healthy and closest failing reading
print("closest pair:", a, b)
print("best threshold:", round((a + b) / 2, 2), " margin:", round((b - a) / 2, 2))
for t in [-0.2, 0.05, 0.3]:                   # other thresholds inside the gap
    print("threshold", t, "-> margin", round(float(np.abs(x - t).min()), 2))

Hard-margin SVM in two features

SVM = widest street between the classes. Street width = 2/‖w‖, so minimise ‖w‖ subject to y(w·x + b) ≥ 1. Only support vectors (on the street edges) decide the line. Hard margin needs separable data; overlap needs the soft margin (C).

Picture

Widest street, touching cars decide. The rings are the cars that touch the line.

Rule of thumb

Small w, wide street. Width is 2/‖w‖, so shrinking ‖w‖ widens the street. "Minimise ‖w‖" and "maximise the margin" are the same instruction.

Read the sign and size

For any point, sign of f(x) is the predicted side and |f(x)| is how far it is from the line (in units of 1/‖w‖). |f| = 1 means exactly on a street edge.

Decode the inequality

y·f(x) ≥ 1 reads as "on the correct side and outside the street" in one go. If y·f is between 0 and 1 the point is correct but inside the street; below 0 it is wrong.

Sanity check

Few support vectors usually means a clean, well-separated problem. If almost every point is a support vector, the classes overlap a lot.

Code to remember: Fit a (nearly) hard-margin linear SVM and read what it learned

import numpy as np
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X = np.array([[1, 1], [3, 3], [4, 3]])   # three customers, two features
y = np.array([-1, 1, 1])                  # -1 = defaulted, +1 = repaid

svm = SVC(kernel="linear", C=1e6)         # huge C: (almost) no violations allowed
svm.fit(X, y)

print("w =", np.round(svm.coef_[0], 3), " b =", round(float(svm.intercept_[0]), 3))

Soft margin: what C does

Soft margin: minimise ½‖w‖² + C·Σξ with slack ξ = max(0, 1 − y·f), the hinge loss. C is a price per unit of violation, not a count. Small C: wide street, many support vectors, tolerates errors. Large C: narrow street, fits single points. On overlapping data it plateaus. Choose C on a log grid by cross-validation.

Picture

Fines for trespass. C is the fine per metre. Cheap fines: wide strip, many trespassers (many support vectors). Dear fines: thin strip, few trespassers.

Formula in words

Cost = (a wish for a wide street) + C × (total rule-breaking). ½‖w‖² + C·Σξ, with ξ = max(0, 1 − y·f).

Sanity check

Compute y·f first. Above 1: slack 0. Between 0 and 1: slack is 1 − y·f, inside the street. Below 0: wrong side, slack above 1. A negative y·f (not a negative f) is the error sign.

Rule of thumb

Search C on a log grid (×10 or ×3 steps), because C acts as a multiplier. If training accuracy is far above cross-validated accuracy, lower C.

Trick

Small C means small w. At C = 0.01 the street is 5.46 wide, wider than the whole cloud of points (4.6 across), so no point reaches an edge. A street that wide is a warning that C is too low.

Hinge loss against log-loss

Margin m = y·f(x). Hinge max(0, 1 − m): zero for m ≥ 1, slope −1 below, so only points in or beyond the street pull (support vectors). Log-loss ln(1 + e^−m): slope −1/(1 + e^m), small but never 0, so every point pulls and σ(f) works as a probability. Same recipe ½‖w‖² + C·Σ loss, same C.

Picture

Hockey stick against a slide. Hinge: flat floor from m = 1, then a straight stick up the left. Log-loss: a smooth slide that flattens towards the floor but never lands.

Formula in words

Both models minimise ½‖w‖² + C × Σ loss(m). Swap the loss and you swap the model: hinge gives the SVM, log-loss gives logistic regression.

Rule of thumb

Pull = slope. Hinge slope: 1 below m = 1, then 0. Log-loss slope: 1/(1 + em), which is roughly e−m for large m, so it shrinks about 2.7 times per unit of margin.

Sanity check

List y·f for your points. Those with y·f < 1 are the ones an SVM listens to. Their count is the support-vector count.

Trick

An SVM score is a distance-like number, not a probability. For probabilities use logistic regression, or calibrate the SVM later (Session 6).

Why SVMs need scaled features

The SVM margin is a Euclidean distance and the penalty is ½‖w‖², and neither is unit-free: divide a feature by s and its weight cost grows by s². A useful feature in tiny numbers gets ignored (58% against 87%). Fix: z = (x − mean)/sd on each column, fitted on training rows only, with make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(...)).

Picture

Same customers, different ruler. Rupees and lakh are two rulers. The SVM measures the street with whichever ruler you hand it. Standardising hands every feature the same ruler: one standard deviation per tick.

Rule of thumb

Any method that uses distances or a penalty on weights (SVM, k-NN, PCA, ridge, logistic with a penalty) needs standardised features. Trees do not.

Formula in words

Divide a feature by s and its weight multiplies by s while its penalty multiplies by s². Units enter the cost squared.

Sanity check

Print X.std(axis=0). If the largest spread is more than about 10 times the smallest, scale. After scaling, the training columns should show mean 0 and sd 1.

Trick

If rescaling does not change the accuracy, do not conclude "scaling is useless". For a linear SVM the gain is modest, but the best C moves with the scale and the RBF kernel (Session 4) does need it.

SVM II: kernels

Lift the number line into a plane

Lift: add computed coordinates, here z → (z, z²), so a straight line in the new space separates what no cut could on the original axis. Fit an ordinary linear SVM there: f = w₁z + w₂z² + b. On the original line f = 0 is a quadratic: two thresholds (−0.967 and 0.917). Half-width (margin) = 1/‖w‖ = 0.612, street = 2/‖w‖. Always lift new data the same way.

Picture

Bend the ruler into a U. The ends climb, the middle stays down, and one flat cut separates them. Straighten the ruler and the cut becomes two marks: z = −0.967 and 0.917.

Rule of thumb

Count the colour changes along the line. Each change needs one threshold, and a line in the (z, z², …, zᵈ) space gives at most d thresholds. Two changes need d = 2. Four colour blocks (three changes) need d = 3.

Sanity check

After lifting, look for an empty gap in the new coordinate. Healthy z² ≤ 0.3025 and failing z² ≥ 1.44 leave a gap, so a line must exist. For a fitted line, every reading on a street edge must give y·f = 1 exactly.

Formula in words

Score = w₁·z + w₂·z² + b. The sign is the class, the zeros of the score are the thresholds, and |score| = 1 marks the street edges.

Link to code

Whatever you do to the training data you must do to new data. Build np.column_stack([z, z**2]) before every predict or decision_function.

Kernels are dot products in a lifted space

Kernel: K(a, b) = φ(a)·φ(b), computed from a and b without building φ. The SVM needs only dot products, so it trains on the Gram matrix K(xᵢ, xⱼ) and scores f(x) = Σ αᵢyᵢ K(xᵢ, x) + b. (ab + r)² lifts to (√(2r)·a, a², r); K(2, 3) = 49 at r = 1. XOR: α = 1/8, f = x₁x₂. RBF = every degree n of (2γab)ⁿ/n!.

Formula in words

K(a, b) is the dot product of the lifted points. For (ab + r)² the lift is (√(2r)·z, z², r). The 2 in √(2r) is what produces the cross term 2r·ab.

Picture

The Gram matrix is a table of how alike each pair of points is. Everything the SVM knows about your data is in that table. Row i, column j: how alike points i and j look in the lifted space.

Sanity check

To trust a kernel claim, compute both routes for one pair. K(2, 3) at r = 1: kernel (6 + 1)² = 49, lifted 12 + 36 + 1 = 49.

Rule of thumb

XOR needs the product x₁x₂ as a coordinate. A degree-2 polynomial kernel contains it, which is why f = x₁x₂ appears.

Trick

Count the lifted coordinates for p inputs and degree d: C(p + d, d). That is 6 for p = 2, d = 2 and 47,905 for p = 64, d = 3. The kernel needs only p multiplications either way.

Kernel playground: C, γ and who gets a vote

The kernel sets the shape family: line, polynomial curve of degree d (use r > 0), or RBF local bumps (width from γ). Diagnose with training minus CV accuracy. Both low, and a better setting exists: underfit, add flexibility. Gap of 10 points or more: overfit, lower γ or C. Start at γ = "scale" = 1/(n_features·Var(X)) and C = 1, then turn one dial at a time.

Picture

Ruler, French curve, freehand pen: the kernel picks the tool. A pen that circles every village is overfit and a ruler through a coastline is underfit.

Sanity check

Training minus CV: below about 3 points is fine, 10 or more is overfit. Both low and equal means underfit, but only if something else scores higher. CV is the number to maximise.

Rule of thumb

Start at γ = "scale", C = 1. Move one dial at a time and by factors of 10, so each move is big enough to see.

Trick

Probe an empty spot between your classes. If the inspector says "b decides" there, γ is far too large: the model has no opinion in the gaps.

Rule of thumb

Support-vector share is high at both extremes of γ, and about a third in the good rows of the moons. But a large C can overfit with fewer supports, so use the gap, not the count.

Formula in words

γ_scale = 1 / (number of features × variance of all entries). Polynomial kernel: (γ·x·z + r) raised to d.

The RBF score as a weighted vote

RBF score: f(z) = Σ αᵢyᵢ exp(−γ(z − zᵢ)²) + b over the support vectors. Each term is a bump of height αᵢyᵢ (never above C in size) and width σ = 1/√(2γ). Sign of f is the class. Far from every support vector f ≈ b. Large γ: narrow spikes, about one voter, b decides the gaps.

Picture

Elders at fixed houses, voices that fade with distance, and a village habit b when nobody is close enough to be heard.

Formula in words

Score = Σ (signed height × closeness) + b. Signed height is α·y (never above C in size). Closeness is exp(−γ × distance²).

Rule of thumb

Compare the width σ = 1/√(2γ) with the spacing of your data. σ much larger than the spacing: too smooth. σ much smaller: every point stands alone. Here readings are 0.2 or more apart, so γ = 1 (σ = 0.71) works and γ = 100 (σ = 0.07) memorises.

Sanity check

A support vector on a street edge must score exactly ±1. If your hand calculation does not give ±1 at a support vector (here f(1.2) = +1), you have an arithmetic slip or a wrong weight.

Trick

Effective voters tells you how local the vote is. On these readings at the probe 1.0 it is 12.0 at γ = 0.01, 2.2 at γ = 1 and 1.0 at γ = 100.

SVM II: regression, anomalies and cost

Support vector regression: the ε-tube

SVR = regression with a tolerance. Loss = max(0, |y − f(x)| − ε): zero inside the tube of half-width ε, linear outside. Minimise ½‖w‖² + C·Σ loss: stay flat, pay C per unit outside. Support vectors are points on or outside the tube; the rest have weight 0. Wider ε: fewer support vectors, flatter curve. ε and C use the units of y, so scale y.

Picture

A hosepipe laid along the curve. Points inside the pipe are invisible to the model. Only the points touching the pipe wall or outside it hold it in place.

Formula in words

Loss = how far outside the tube you are, never below 0: max(0, |r| − ε). Inside is free; outside you pay by the unit.

Rule of thumb

Pick ε from the noise you accept. On this page's sine data (noise standard deviation 0.22) an ε between 0.1 and 0.2 gave the best fit to the true curve on average over 40 samples; ε = 0.5 doubled the error.

Sanity check

After fitting, every point without a ring must lie inside the tube, and every point outside the tube must have a ring. If not, the solver has not converged.

Trick

ε and C live in the units of y. If y is in rupees, ε = 0.1 is zero and C = 1 is tiny. Standardise y (the code below does this with TransformedTargetRegressor) before tuning.

One-class SVM and ν

One-class SVM = describe the normal data, flag the rest. Score f(x) = Σ αᵢ·exp(−γ‖x − xᵢ‖²) − ρ (positive normal, negative unusual). Weights αᵢ are in [0, 1] and sum to νn, so outside ≤ ν ≤ support vectors. ν is a ceiling, not the false-alarm rate. Large γ memorises: zero flagged in training, many on new data.

Picture

A landscape of hills on support vectors. The black line is the contour at height ρ. Raise γ and the hills become narrow spikes; raise ν and the water level ρ rises, leaving more points on dry ground (outside).

Formula in words

Score = sum of weighted bumps minus ρ. Weights are in [0, 1] and add up to νn.

Rule of thumb

outside ≤ ν ≤ support vectors. Both sides use the same number νn, so the share outside can never exceed ν and the share of support vectors can never fall below it.

Sanity check

If almost every point is a support vector and none is outside, γ is too large. The model has memorised the training set.

Rule of thumb

Start with a small γ (0.1 to 1 for standardised features) and raise it only while held-out normal data stays unflagged. Then use ν to set how many training points you accept as odd. Scale the features first, because γ is measured against distance.

Training cost grows roughly with n² (and what to use instead)

Kernel SVM training ≈ n² (the n × n Gram matrix): double the rows, about 4 times the time; 8n² bytes if stored; prediction ≈ support vectors × features. Logistic regression and trees scale about linearly. Rule: under 10k rows anything; 10k to 100k baseline and trees first; over 100k skip the kernel SVM. Run a scaled logistic regression first; keep the cheapest model within one SE of the best.

Picture

The Gram matrix is a square table. Double the rows and the table is twice as wide and twice as tall: four times as many cells. A linear model only walks down one column of data.

Rule of thumb

Size gate. Up to about 10,000 rows, any model is cheap. From 10,000 to 100,000, try linear models and trees first, and tune the kernel SVM on a subsample. Above 100,000, skip the kernel SVM (or use Nystroem features with a linear model). These are defaults to test on your machine.

Rule of thumb

Shape gate. Run a scaled logistic regression first. If the best model beats it by less than one standard error, keep the linear model. If it scores near 50% on a balanced problem, the boundary is not straight, so move to an SVM or trees.

Rule of thumb

Data gate. Text and other sparse data with many features: linear. Mixed or categorical columns, missing values, unscaled features: trees or a forest. Dense, numeric, curved, under about 100,000 rows: compare an RBF SVM and a forest.

Trick

Extrapolate before you launch. Time a tenth of the data and multiply by 100 (the exponent is about 2). Then multiply by the number of fits: a 5 × 5 grid with 5-fold CV is 125.

Sanity check

A kernel SVM that is only 0.3 points better than logistic regression, but 100 times slower, is a bad trade. Always write the fit time next to the accuracy.

One test set is one draw of the lottery

One hold-out score is one draw: SE = √(p(1−p)/m), 8 points for 20 test rows. K-fold tests every row once; repeating it with new shuffles removes the luck of the cut (sd 1.1 down to 0.6 points on the page). Neither removes the luck of the sample: only more rows do (about 327 test rows for SE = 2 points). Steadier is not truer.

Picture

A 20-question quiz from a 100-question bank. One quiz is a draw. Five quizzes covering the whole bank, averaged, are steady. But a poor bank gives a poor average whatever you do.

Formula in words

SE = √(p(1−p)/m). Four times the test rows, half the wobble. At p near 0.85: 20 rows give 8 points, 100 rows 3.6, 1,000 rows 1.1.

Rule of thumb

Before you trust a score, ask how many rows were behind it? With fewer than about 100 test rows, a difference of 5 points between two models can be luck.

Rule of thumb

Report 5-fold (or repeated 5-fold) instead of one hold-out for small data. Use repeated K-fold to remove the luck of the cut. Use more rows to remove the luck of the sample.

Sanity check

If your score changes by more than 1 or 2 points when you only change the random seed of the split, the estimate is not stable enough to compare models with.

Grid, random and smart search under a budget

Tuning is a search under a trial budget. Grid: g values per knob, g^k trials, only g different values per knob. Random: every trial new; P(hit a good region of share q) = 1 − (1 − q)^n. TPE (Optuna) learns where good trials were. On two knobs they tie within about a point; random wins when knobs are irrelevant. best_score_ is still optimistic: use nested CV.

Picture

Look at the dots from the side. A 3 × 3 grid projects onto only 3 values of C. 9 random points project onto 9. Count the different values per axis, not the dots.

Formula in words

P(hit) = 1 − (1 − q)n. If the good region is q of the space, n random trials all miss with probability (1 − q)n. About 30 trials find a top-10% setting 96% of the time. About 60 find a top-5% setting 95% of the time.

Rule of thumb

Log scale for C, γ and learning rates. A plain uniform draw between 0.01 and 1,000 puts 99% of draws above 10 and almost never tries small values.

Rule of thumb

Choose by situation. One or two knobs and cheap trials: a grid is fine. Many knobs, few of which matter: random. Expensive trials or a narrow good region: TPE (Optuna).

Sanity check

Is the best value on the edge of your range? If the best C is the largest one you offered, the true best may be outside. Widen the range and search again.

Model evaluation II: calibration, decisions and comparing models

Does a 70% forecast come true 70% of the time?

Calibrated: of cases forecast p, a share p happen. Reliability diagram: one dot per bin at (average forecast, observed share); the diagonal is honest, flatter is over-confident. ECE = Σ (n_b/n)|observed − forecast|. Brier = mean (forecast − outcome)²; log-loss punishes confident mistakes harder. Zero ECE alone proves nothing: always check Brier too.

Picture

Dots on the diagonal are honest. Flatter than the diagonal is over-confident: the model shouts "0.97" and reality says 0.93. Steeper than the diagonal is under-confident: it whispers "0.74" and reality says 0.88.

Rule of thumb

Look at the bars before the dots. A dot with a tiny bar under it is noise. The biggest bar usually decides the ECE, so ask "how many cases?" before "how far from the diagonal?".

Sanity check

The lazy forecaster who says the overall repay rate ȳ for everyone has an ECE of about 0 and a Brier score of ȳ(1 − ȳ), which is 0.163 on the default data. A real model must beat that Brier score, so never judge ECE alone.

Formula in words

ECE = average gap, weighted by how many cases. Brier = average squared miss. Log-loss = average surprise at what really happened.

Trick

AUC hides dishonesty, because it only sees order. Naive Bayes has AUC 0.81 against 0.82 for logistic regression, yet ECE 0.085 against 0.018. Draw the diagram for your own model; never assume.

Fixing the probabilities: Platt scaling and isotonic regression

Calibrator = map from raw score to honest probability, learned on unseen rows. Platt: p = 1/(1+e^−(a·score+b)), two numbers, best with few rows or S-shaped error. Isotonic: rising staircase by pooling adjacent violators, best with 1,000+ rows. Platt keeps AUC exactly; isotonic may create ties. Never fit or score on the rows the model learned from.

Picture

Platt is a dial: two knobs, a (stretch) and b (shift), one smooth S. Isotonic is a staircase: as many steps as the data support, never down. Few rows, use the dial. Many rows, the staircase.

Rule of thumb

Under about 200 calibration rows, or when the model is naive Bayes, start with Platt. From about 1,000 rows, isotonic usually wins on Brier and calibration error. Between, try both and compare on rows neither has seen.

Trick

Read Platt's a as a verdict. a below 1: the model shouted, shrink it (naive Bayes 0.33). a above 1: it whispered, stretch it (SVM 1.72). a near 1 and b near 0: leave it alone.

Sanity check

After calibrating, the AUC must not fall for Platt (it is a rising curve). If Platt's AUC changed, the fitted a was zero or negative, which usually means too few calibration rows.

Rule of thumb

Never score a calibrator on the rows it learned from. Isotonic looks best there because it can memorise them: 0.117 against 0.168 for Platt on the ten example rows.

Pick the cut-off from the costs, not from 0.5

Approve if expected profit p·G − (1 − p)·L is above 0, that is p above L / (G + L). With G = 1,000 and L = 9,000: 0.90 (odds 9 to 1). Cost ratios 1:1, 3:1, 9:1, 19:1 give 0.50, 0.75, 0.90, 0.95. It needs calibrated p: an over-confident model needs a higher raw cut-off. The profit peak on finite data is noisy; the formula is the target.

Picture

Draw the straight line expected profit = (G + L)·p − L. It starts at −L when p = 0 and reaches +G when p = 1. The cut-off is where it crosses zero.

Rule of thumb

Approve when the odds of repayment beat L : G. Loss 9 times the gain means odds above 9 to 1, which is 90%. Loss 3 times the gain means 3 to 1, which is 75%.

Sanity check

Raise the loss and the cut-off must rise. If your formula gives 0.10 for gain 1,000 and loss 9,000, you have gain and loss swapped: it would approve almost everyone.

Formula in words

Cut-off = what you lose divided by what you win plus what you lose. At equal stakes, half. As the loss grows the cut-off creeps towards 1 but never reaches it.

Trick

Always check calibration before using the formula. If the probabilities are over-confident, the best raw cut-off sits above L / (G + L): naive Bayes averaged 0.96 over 20 datasets instead of 0.90.

Is 86% really better than 85%?

Test accuracy has SE = √(p(1−p)/n). The gap between two models on separate rows has SD √(SE_A² + SE_B²), about √2 × SE for equals; 95% of gaps lie within ±1.96 SD (±3.1 points at 85%, n = 1,000). Quadruple n to halve it. A true 1-point gap is reversed about 1 time in 4 at 1,000 rows. One test set: bootstrap interval; containing 0 means "cannot tell".

Formula in words

SE = square root of (p times q over n), where q = 1 − p. For a gap between two models on separate rows, multiply by √2.

Rule of thumb

Quadruple the rows to halve the noise. Near 85% accuracy the SD of the gap between two models is about 50 / √n points: 1.6 at n = 1,000, 0.5 at n = 10,000.

Sanity check

Before trusting a gap, compare it with 2 × SD(gap). At 1,000 rows near 85% that is about 3 points. A smaller gap on one test set is not evidence.

Picture

Draw the bell curve of gaps with a mark at the true gap and a line at 0. The area to the left of the line is the chance the worse model wins. Narrower bell (more rows) or bigger true gap means less area.

Trick

"Interval contains 0" means "this test set cannot tell", not "the models are equal". Absence of proof is not proof of absence: with a true 1-point gap, 9 draws in 10 contain 0.

Only the rows where the models disagree matter

Same test rows for both models. b = only A right, c = only B right. Equal models make each disagreement a fair coin: exact p = 2 · P(X ≤ min(b, c)), X ~ Binomial(b + c, ½); in scipy, binomtest(b, b + c).pvalue. Quick check: (|b − c| − 1) / √(b + c) above 2 means p below 0.05. Agreeing rows add nothing. Large p: "cannot tell".

Picture

Two tally columns. Make a mark for every row where only A is right and for every row where only B is right. Ignore all other rows. Then ask: is this tally lopsided enough that a fair coin would rarely do it?

Rule of thumb

The square root of the chi-square statistic is a z-score: (|b − c| − 1) / √(b + c). Above about 2 means p below about 0.05. Check: 31 v 23 gives 0.95 (p = 0.34); 12 v 3 gives 2.07 (p = 0.039); 300 v 250 gives 2.09 (p = 0.037).

Rule of thumb

The z-score grows like √(b + c). At the same proportions, doubling z needs 4 times the disagreements: b = 60, c = 50 has z = 0.86, and reaching z above 2 took 5 times as many (300 v 250), not a few more rows.

Sanity check

The four cells must add up to the number of test rows, and swapping A and B must leave p unchanged (only the sign of the gap flips). If b + c = 0 there is nothing to test.

Trick

Large p means "cannot tell", not "equal". Read the interval as well: −0.7 to +2.2 on the default data still allows A to be 2 points better.

The paired t-test across CV folds fools you

Repeated K-fold gives J = K × R differences d that re-use the same rows. The naive paired t uses SE² = s²/J and cries wolf (21.7% with K = 5, R = 5 on equal models). Nadeau and Bengio: SE² = s²(1/J + n_test/n_train), with n_test/n_train = 1/(K − 1); same as t_naive ÷ √(1 + J · n_test/n_train). With one test set, use McNemar instead.

Formula in words

Corrected t = naive t ÷ √(1 + J × n_test/n_train). For K = 5 and J = 25 the divisor is 2.69. Use it to check any CV t-test you read.

Rule of thumb

In K-fold, n_test/n_train = 1/(K − 1): 1 for K = 2, 0.25 for K = 5, 0.11 for K = 10. For huge J the corrected variance floors at s² × n_test/n_train, so with K = 5 repeats can never push the SE below 0.5 s.

Picture

Under the 25 dots there are only 300 rows, and about 21 of them ever disagree (page default, first 100 comparisons). That is the real sample, whatever J is. Count rows, not folds.

Rule of thumb

Repeats polish the estimate, not the evidence. They smooth out the luck of one random split, so d̄ is steadier, but they cannot remove the uncertainty of having only n rows.

Sanity check

To audit any comparison procedure, run it on a model against an equal twin, many times. Honest means about 5% flagged and a flat p-value histogram.

Code to remember: Naive and corrected t from five fold differences

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
d = np.array([2, 1, 0, 1, 1]) / 60              # A minus B accuracy in each fold (60 test rows per fold)
J, ratio = len(d), 60 / 240                     # number of differences, n_test / n_train
t_naive = d.mean() / np.sqrt(d.var(ddof=1) / J)
t_corr = d.mean() / np.sqrt(d.var(ddof=1) * (1 / J + ratio))
for name, t in [("naive", t_naive), ("corrected", t_corr)]:
    print(name, "t =", round(t, 2), "p =", round(2 * stats.t.sf(abs(t), df=J - 1), 3))

Same accuracy, different errors by group

Per group, from tp, fp, fn, tn: approval (tp+fp)/n; TPR tp/(tp+fn); FPR fp/(fp+tn); precision tp/(tp+fp); accuracy (tp+tn)/n. Ask "out of whom?". Different score histograms and base rates make one cut-off give unequal rates. Equal accuracy can hide unequal errors. Different base rates mean not every rate can be equal. Dropping a column leaves proxies. Which to equalise is policy.

Trick

For every rate ask "out of whom?" Approval rate: everyone in the group. TPR: those who would repay. FPR: those who would default. Precision: those we approved. Accuracy: everyone. Nearly all mix-ups are a wrong denominator.

Formula in words

A group earns money when precision is above loss / (gain + loss), here 9,000 / 10,000 = 0.90. Check: B's precision 90.6% gave +62,000; at 83.4% B lost 1,025,000.

Rule of thumb

Accuracy is the weakest kind of equality: false positives and false negatives can trade against each other. Look at the pair (FPR, TPR), or at all four cells, never at accuracy alone.

Formula in words

Precision = TPR·π / (TPR·π + FPR·(1 − π)). Same TPR and FPR but a lower base rate π means lower precision. A's rates with B's π give 85.8% instead of 92.0%.

Picture

Two histograms, one line each. Sliding B's line changes four numbers at once. Each "Make one measure equal" button says: slide B's line until this number matches A's, then read the rest.

Sanity check

For each group tp + fp + fn + tn must equal n (2,174 + 189 + 719 + 527 = 3,609), and the "All" cells must equal A plus B. If a rate does not recompute from the cells, a cell is mislabelled.

Trick

To look for proxies, try to predict the group from the other columns. AUC clearly above 0.5 means the columns carry group information: 0.67 on this table, 0.60 even after dropping income, balance and savings.

def rates(tp, fp, fn, tn):
    n = tp + fp + fn + tn
    return {"approval": (tp + fp) / n, "TPR": tp / (tp + fn), "FPR": fp / (fp + tn),
            "precision": tp / (tp + fp), "accuracy": (tp + tn) / n}
